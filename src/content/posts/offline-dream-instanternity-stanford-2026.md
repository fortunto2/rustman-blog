# Bara Kolenc at Stanford: we are authors and spectators of our own lives, and the internet keeps the gap from fading

I asked Bara Kolenc whether we are the directors of our own lives or only the audience. "We are both," she said, and the internet and AI did not create that split, they exposed it. The gap between the one who directs a life and the one who watches it is what produces anxiety. While it stays in our heads it is transient; put online, it stays. Kolenc, Gregor Moder and Jure Simoniti, philosophers from the University of Ljubljana, spoke at Stanford on 27 October 2026 in a panel called ["The Offline Dream Program: Three Takes on Film, the Internet, and Instanternity"](https://creees.stanford.edu/events/offline-dream-program-three-takes-film-internet-and-instanternity), moderated by Pavle Levi of Stanford's Department of Art & Art History. I was in the audience, and since I build [[project-life2film]], a personal AI film director, the question was not abstract for me.

## The short version

- **The moment is a cut.** Kolenc opened with a riddle about crows and a gunshot: the moment is not the smallest piece of duration but a switch that creates reality and time at once.
- **Six definitions of the web**, from "materialized virtuality" to the ideological apparatus that has replaced the school, ending on her book's thesis: a different internet is possible.
- **Every post is capital.** Even a post against capitalism feeds it, she said, and the web's one remaining ideology is consumption for its own sake.
- **The AI question changes.** Not "is it conscious?" but "is it a split subject?": does it doubt, is it built on desire.
- **History survived its own death.** Moder recalled the 1990s: genocide in Bosnia next door while people talked about the end of history.
- **Film cannot not narrate.** Simoniti: every next shot resolves the tension of the previous one, and still leaves an image no story can absorb.

## The evening

The panel belonged to the three philosophers' "Repetition and Contingency" series across US universities. Campus power outages moved it from Encina Commons to Oshman Hall in the McMurtry Building. Moder had spoken before I started recording, on Lubitsch and Shakespeare; then came Simoniti on image and narrative, then Kolenc; Levi questioned each of them and then opened the floor. My transcript starts in the last minutes of Simoniti's talk, so Moder's talk appears here through the official abstract and his answers.

## Bara Kolenc: welcome to instanternity

Her talk, "Welcome to Instanternity: Who's the Real Alien Today?", condensed her book *The Internet and the Subject: Reflections on Desire, Ideology, and Time in the Age of Instanternity* (Analecta, 2025, in Slovenian). She showed no clips: she was speaking about the internet, and "we are on our screens all the time anyway". She said she could have zoomed in on one theme, mass psychosis in the age of AI, reading AI as mimicry the way psychotics, in Lacan's account, lean on imaginary identifications. She zoomed out instead, to the book's ontological core. Her abstract frames it with "three impossible things": "a pervert without a body. Consumerism without desire. And ideology without a sublime object." The real question of posthumanism, it says, is not whether the human disappears but "If the human goes extinct, will the subject survive?"

### A gunshot, five crows, and the moment as a cut

A shot is fired, one crow falls. How many are still sitting? By deduction, four, if there were five. By experience, none. Logic relies on the consistency of reality, she said, but reality is inconsistent: the crows fly off, and a moment later they may be back on the wire in a different order.

The moment, for her, is a cut that slices into reality and changes it radically and unpredictably, a gunshot that establishes reality and time together. It both is and is not, and it cannot be the smallest possible duration, because it is the switch between duration and non-duration. So time should be thought not from a continuous line, which only gives an appearance of consistency, but from the indeterminacy of the moment. Reality is a constellation established anew at every moment: not a mystical world crumbling into dust and rebuilt, not an illusion, but reality *for the subject*, and dialectical rather than flat.

Anyone who edits hears this literally. A cut is not a short piece of time; it is where one stretch ends and the next begins, and the viewer's sense of what happened is built from those switches. The crows back on the wire in a new order is also what a montage does to a day.

### Six definitions of the web

**1. A realm of reality.** The web is not an artificial world split off from "true" everyday reality; it is part of the reality we relate to, and it has shown us how reality works. At the same time it feeds our fantasy of reality as a consistent whole to which we delegate meaning, the picture we call the world. That is why we like the idea of a *global* web.

**2. A materialization of virtuality.** The virtual, in philosophy, exists as an idea not yet actualized. On the web virtuality is externalized and actualized, material in its composition and above all in its effects, which are concrete and physical. Life2Film began from the everyday version: most of the video we record sits unwatched, virtual memory stored on very real hardware.

**3. A medium that reverses McLuhan.** For McLuhan media were extensions of man; with the web, man becomes an extension of the medium. She read the meeting of digital and non-digital through Debord's "reciprocal alienation" of reality and spectacle: reality belongs to the spectacle and the spectacle is real. So the pre-digital turns into a myth that we cannot define without the digital.

**4. A machine.** She cited Imperva's 2025 report: 53% of all activity on the internet was automated, and with AI agents, she said, the share grows exponentially. First to debunk is the post-scarcity dream of robots working while we lie in the sun, a dream as old as Daedalus and the self-moving tripods of Hephaestus and always the fantasy of a master served by robotic slaves. Marx took it apart in *Capital*, and she quoted the sentence where machinery becomes "the most unfailing means for placing every moment of the labourer's time and that of his family, at the disposal of the capitalist" (Moore and Aveling's translation). What freed people from labor was never the machine, but the socio-economic system around it. She was clear this does not mean going backwards: nothing is wrong with technology as such, and it could serve the ideals of the Enlightenment and a just, sustainable economy. What we need to see through is the techno-ideology that treats its development as naturally embedded in capitalism.

Then Marx's law of the moral depreciation of machinery. Labor time measures value, so the faster a commodity is made, the less the work and the product are worth. A machine first embodies the labor it replaces, a worker's week done in a minute; once it runs, its value is set by the labor time needed to reproduce the machine itself. Her reading draws two consequences. The machine is a "peculiar amphibian", both labor power and product, and so is the web powered by AI agents. And it carries an internal necessity to reproduce itself faster, a law of acceleration Marx found long before Moore's law and accelerationism. Anyone who has watched a model or a GPU lose its price in months because a cheaper one shipped has seen moral depreciation at work.

**5. An ideological apparatus.** For Althusser the school replaced the church as the modern state's dominant ideological apparatus; her claim is that the web has replaced the school. Online every creed and politics seems to have room, master signifiers roam, ideologies seem to multiply without end, and in that very appearance of plurality the web becomes a privileged field of enjoyment, desire and the big Other. Its cunning: the more redundant a person becomes in its mechanism, the more dependent on it they are, and the more they enjoy it. Even views critical of capitalism stay hypocritical towards their own stance once posted, because every post is capital and feeds its accumulation. The sole ideology left is consumerism, emptied of any concrete idea: consumption for consumption, wealth for wealth, power for power, capital for capital. An "ideology of ideology", a pure tautology.

**6. One form of the internet.** The global web as we know it is only one particular form of the internet. Hence the book's founding proposition: in search of what Badiou calls egalitarian emancipation, start from the claim that "a different internet is possible".

### Instanternity, and time that is not ours

Instanternity, her coinage from *instant* and *eternity*, names both the change in how we experience time under digitalization and a new temporal regime of the internet and AI, in which temporality becomes a force shaping material reality. Her example: what happens to Ukraine when Musk turns off Starlink for a day.

She separates time itself, the original exposition of time, from what she calls, after Heidegger, vulgar time: time as humans experience and represent it, to which social time and history belong. For her, as for Heidegger, the original exposition of time is the moment. But where, in her reading, Heidegger's moment opens onto pure presence, hers is an intervention, a cut, a shot, temporal and extra-temporal at once, producing presence and absence together. Back to the crows.

### Four forms of time that live side by side

The book puts forward five historicizing hypotheses, which she called its "vulgar part", since they belong to vulgar time themselves. She had time for two. The first: instanternity has succeeded postmodernity, because digital technology appears to realize postmodernity's collapse of temporal and historical distinctions. Whether modernity ends with it is open: modernity rests on Galilean science, Enlightenment rationality, the modern state and the Cartesian subject, and losing even one, the state or the divided subject, would signal its end.

The second is a model of four forms of vulgar time, names her own:

1. **Immersive time**, the time of nature and pure presence, which she tied to *kairos* and deep time. Prehistory; its quality is timelessness.
2. **Current time**, the linear arrow of clocks, calendars and physics, *chronos*, cutting into immersive time step by step. That is history. Pre-modernity; steady, uniform time.
3. **Valorized current time**: in modernity time is measured and turned into value, as labor time. Its quality is acceleration.
4. A fourth form, the "valorized exponential convergence toward the limit" of current time (its name was unclear on my recording). It belongs to a digital revolution we are only on the threshold of, its quality is simultaneity, and in it history becomes one stratum among others rather than the measure of everything.

Each new form coexists with the earlier ones. Beside them all sits time as such, outside history, and non-temporal structures that show up as topologies, networks, patterns and relations; her examples were particle entanglement and what Michael Levin calls bioelectricity. One reading note: in most Greek usage *kairos* is the opportune moment, so take her definition of immersive time from her.

### "It thinks", and who the real alien is

She closed on two words, "it thinks". We assume it describes AI, she said, and that is wrong: it describes something more dangerous, more powerful and closer to us, the subject. The subject is not what neuroscience, neuropsychology or behaviorism posit, neither the self nor self-reflection. The self exists, but, in what she called one of Freud's most important discoveries, it is an object to the subject, a construct of our desires and fantasies about who we are. Subjectivity is not consciousness; we are not in full control of our desires, thoughts or deeds. "But if our thoughts are fundamentally alienated from us, who then is the real alien today?"

That line stays with me. A film an AI makes of my day is, in her terms, the self: an image of who I think I am, built from desire and fantasy. The subject is whoever watches it and does not quite coincide with it.

### Doubt, desire, and an AI that would have to be split

Levi said he loves the Ljubljana reading of the Cogito, the thing that thinks, though it costs a radical reduction to the thing minus the self. Then a joke, as I heard it: Descartes found his method shut in a heated room, the *poêle*; isn't such a box only waiting for a pinhole to become a camera obscura?

Kolenc answered that the subject of psychoanalysis is Cartesian, because it begins not where thought meets itself but where thought is put in doubt. Doubt defines the subject, so to ask whether an AI could be one, look at whether it doubts and how it acts. And Lacan: *desidero* is the Freudian cogito, the subject is built on desire. Then the question about AI becomes whether it is a split subject. Split subjects are not consistent with themselves, "not at home", always dependent on others, so intersubjectivity is the founding condition. It is also, she said, the fundamental proposition of the internet, which is or was a society, with the potential to be not only a medium of communication but society itself transposed into a new medium. "But what is it now? It is a capitalist machine."

### The internet is not the web

So she separates the internet, the underlying protocols by which computers connect, from the World Wide Web, which from 1991, in her account, took that structure over and monopolized it. Imagine the internet without the web and you get a different future: "internet that is not a consumption machine". Among its material effects she listed the materials and energy it needs, its effects on our psyche and brains, and on the environment.

An audience member read the whole panel as one story about representation: from authorship (a provincial actor playing Hitler, Shakespeare), through what Deleuze and Guattari would call geophilosophy, Europe against America, to the web as pure property, representation turned commodity. Kolenc took up property. Authorship is a modern invention tied to ownership and copyright, and "we don't own ideas": they should be held in common. On the web as spectacle, we end up like marbles that reflect each other without really connecting.

### The books behind the talk

The talk condensed the Slovenian book, but her English one, *Repetition and Subjectivity: Kierkegaard, Freud, Lacan* (SUNY Press, 2026), is the theory underneath it, and it explains the name of the series. Per the publisher's description, she starts from repetition as an ontological principle of duality and lays out four matrices of repetition, deflation, reformation, inflation and formation, the last of which underlies the modern subject. Kierkegaard articulated it first; Freud and Lacan gave its logic its clearest form. Along the way she reworks the Greek *anámnesis* (remembering), the death drive and *jouissance*, and treats repetition as the central mechanism of the subject's relation to reality. Her earlier books, in Slovenian, are *Figura dvojnika: Od komedije k psihozi* (The Figure of the Double: From Comedy to Psychosis) and *Ponavljanje in uprizoritev: Kierkegaard, psihoanaliza, gledališče* (Repetition and Staging: Kierkegaard, Psychoanalysis, Theatre), and she co-edited *Master/s* with Moder and Simoniti.

Re-watching your own edited day is a repetition in exactly this sense. In Kierkegaard's *Repetition*, Constantin Constantius goes back to Berlin to repeat a trip and finds it cannot be done, and the book sets repetition against recollection: recollection repeats backward, genuine repetition is "recollected forward". A film of a day is recollection. The open question for a tool like mine is whether watching it again can ever work forward.

## I asked: are we the directors of our lives, or the audience?

I said I am building Life2Film, a personal AI film director: you shoot video through the day, and it makes a film of your life automatically. Then I asked two things. Are we the directors of our lives, or just the audience? And now that AI edits the video, who is the director, still me or the machine?

Kolenc took the first half: we are both, "the authors and the spectators at the same time", and the internet, digitalization and AI only exposed what we already were. We keep directing our lives through a fantasy of who we are and where we stand, projecting what our life will be, and we are always outside it too, watching. That split produces the anxiety, the pressure. In our heads it is transient and it works. Materialized online, it stays, and that, she suggested, is probably why we have so much more anxiety and so many more psychic problems.

She did not take up the machine. Her answer still bears on it. If AI only exposes a split we already had, the editing machine is not a rival director; it puts the old gap between the one who lives and the one who watches on a screen, and makes it last. A director that records everything and plays it all back widens the stored half of that gap. So it should help us forget as well as remember, keeping the few shots that carry the day and letting the rest go. And if every post is capital, a film of someone's life should stay private by default, not raw material for a platform. That is the old rule of [[privacy-as-architecture]]: if we cannot see your footage, we cannot leak it or sell it. The Life2Film desktop app runs everything on the Mac and uploads nothing; the web version keeps the originals on the device and sends only low-resolution proxies for analysis. We can hand the editing to a machine, but not the memories.

## Gregor Moder: the theatricality of power

Moder's talk was "Lubitsch, Shakespeare and the Theatricality of Power". Per the abstract, Lubitsch's *To Be or Not to Be* (1942) follows a theatre troupe in occupied Poland and asks what makes a convincing performance; early critics found its human, joking Nazis disrespectful. For Moder political action and the metaphysics of performance are linked, as in Shakespeare, and "there is no defeating Nazism without a thorough understanding of the theatricality of power as such".

Levi pushed it backwards in time: if Lubitsch teaches us about Shakespeare, what does Shakespeare teach about Lubitsch, and what can Hitler teach us about his impersonators? "I hope not very much," Moder said, before calling it the clever question. Lubitsch and Chaplin both knew that fascism has an affinity with theatricality and exists through public performance of its program; he mentioned a paper finding the same element in how Milošević held power. The open question is whether that theatricality has changed. We learned to talk about power with ideas from the Second World War and from Shakespeare, and a figure like Donald Trump, "very hard to avoid", makes a claim to power that is similar and also quite different.

### History survived its own death

On temporality he spoke personally. The 1990s were painful for him: war and genocide near his own country, mostly in Bosnia, while people spoke of the end of history. Fukuyama had argued that after the Soviet collapse liberal democracy was the best and only way to live, so wars had no reason left. "And yet, you know, there were." Steven Pinker kept arguing that war has receded. History does not work that way, Moder said; the end of history of the 90s was pure fantasy, and history "survived its own death pretty well". Someone noted that Fukuyama has since changed his view.

### Comedy against the cult of the leader

Later he added what he had no time for in the talk: why comedy handles the authority figure so well. The Führer insists on being the one unique figure who holds the nation together, and because everything is invested in him, comedy can show at once that he is "just a random guy with a mustache". One of the things that pushed Chaplin to make *The Great Dictator*, he said, was that he wore the same mustache: wait, I could be that guy. That is why, for him, the best films on how Nazism worked are comedies; list Lubitsch and Chaplin and you already have a line. How representation works today, he called "very volatile", without claiming an answer.

Kolenc has a book on that figure, *Figura dvojnika* (The Figure of the Double: From Comedy to Psychosis). Going by the title alone, it spans the evening: the double as comedy in Chaplin's barber who passes for the dictator, the double as the psychotic's imaginary identification she brought up about AI. Deepfakes and AI avatars have made the double cheap, which makes the question of which way it falls, comedy or psychosis, a practical one.

### Shylock, cut before revenge

The last question to him: Lubitsch, a European liberal of his generation, reads Shylock's monologue as a humanist declaration, but in the play it is nastier, tied to the pound of flesh. Moder agreed. In *The Merchant of Venice* the speech answers the question of what Shylock wants with the flesh at all, and his answer is that it will feed his revenge on Antonio, who has been cruel to him throughout. The Nazis staged their own Shylocks; later Jews and Israelis reclaimed him as a man wronged. Whether the play is antisemitic or plays with antisemitism is, as so often with Shakespeare, unclear.

What matters for the film, he said, is that Lubitsch cuts the monologue off before the revenge: eyes, organs, senses, passions, and never the line about revenge. *Hamlet*, the film's other reference, is a failed revenge tragedy. Yet even in Shylock, he said, revenge is not a Jewish eye for an eye set against Christian mercy. Shakespeare's point is that "even in revenge we are just like you".

## Jure Simoniti: film cannot not narrate

Simoniti's abstract defines film as "the art of the transcendental, that is, irresolvable dialectic between image and narrative": "American cinema employs the image to produce a narrative, while European cinema employs narrative to produce an image." The burning sled of *Citizen Kane* stitches the film into one story; the dead fish at the end of Fellini's *La dolce vita* stays an icon. European "woman's films" such as Bergman's *Persona* or Chéreau's *Queen Margot* raise the woman into an Icon no narrative resolves.

What I heard was the end of *Queen Margot*. Adjani's face, he said, does not show the stoic internalization of loss that Garbo shows in *Queen Christina*. She suffers, a tear rolls, yet her lover's death cannot be written into her face as psychology or biography. La Mole's death is "the price the narrative pays" to give back the innocence her face lost at the wedding. The severed head in her lap stages two faces, one carrying the weight of the story, one standing above it; her younger brother opposite is the pure spectator of her elevation into an Icon. She rises from the ashes of her narrative "as an image through which no story can any longer pass".

### The moderator's challenge: does closure fail?

Levi's question was the sharpest of the evening. Simoniti's narrative examples were classical Hollywood, where narrative means closure. But take Ethan at the end of *The Searchers*, left outside as the door shuts. In a thought experiment, a habit of the Ljubljana school he said he loves, a European director might have ended on Ethan walking away. Wouldn't that lingering image belong more to the narrative than one cut off so abruptly? And isn't the burning Rosebud in *Citizen Kane* a failed closure, since learning that it was a sled is not enough? Even insistent Hollywood closure may leave images beyond it.

Simoniti answered with what he had not had time for. A film is a sequence of images, and one image following another is already a minimum of narrative, so film "cannot not narrate". But what makes film film is that the next shot always resolves a tension contained in the previous one. So it always produces an "emblematic surplus" over the narrative, and neither side cancels the other. Is the end of *The Searchers* beautiful because it puts the story to rest, or because of the lone man in the doorway? Probably the image, he said.

That is the Kuleshov effect seen from the editing table, and the most practical thing I heard all evening. Every cut asks and the next shot answers, so a machine that orders clips is already telling a story whether it means to or not.

### Endings inside time and outside it

The last audience question: in his American examples the resolution falls on one discrete moment, the sled catching fire, while *Queen Margot*, *The 400 Blows* and *Run Lola Run* fade or freeze. Is the resolution there outside of time? He partly agreed. Narrative cinema ties its ending to the story and puts it into time; the "European touch" leaves an atemporal icon at the end. The historical Marguerite de Valois lived on and wrote her *Memoirs*, published in 1628, which are mostly what she is remembered for. The film still ends on the icon: the story is brought to a halt, not by being resolved, but by being revealed.

That gap is the one a personal film has to respect. A life goes on and writes its own story; a cut freezes it into someone else's. The film of a day can make an icon of a moment, but the person in it should stay the Margot who writes the memoirs.

## What I take back to the editing room

My reading, not theirs:

- **Every cut asks and answers.** Order is story, so the sequence matters more than any single clip.
- **Keep one shot the story cannot explain.** Simoniti's emblematic surplus is what people remember, and an editor tuned only for a clean story will cut it.
- **Choose where the ending sits**: inside time, on an event like the burning sled, or outside it, on a freeze frame or a fade. They make different films.
- **No feed, no hook.** If the web's cunning is that we come to enjoy our own dependence, a personal film app should not borrow its tricks; that is the anti-exploitation line in my [[manifest-summary]]: no dark patterns, no addiction mechanics.
- **Forget as well as remember**, and keep the film on the owner's device unless they decide otherwise ([[privacy-first]]).

## Who goes further on this

Kolenc's talk was a map of a whole book in twenty minutes. These writers go deeper on single roads across it, often with more cases and data than a talk can carry.

- **Bernard Stiegler**, *Technics and Time* (3 vols, 1994–2001), especially vol. 3, *Cinematic Time and the Question of Malaise*. His tertiary retention is memory externalized in technical objects, from writing to film, and he reads consciousness itself as selecting and editing like cinema. The closest thinker to Life2Film, and more specific than the talk about how externalized memory reshapes the one who remembers.
- **José van Dijck**, *Mediated Memories in the Digital Age* (2007). Personal memory made with photos, home video and music rather than stored in them. More empirical and closer to the family camera roll than to ontology.
- **Viktor Mayer-Schönberger**, *Delete: The Virtue of Forgetting in the Digital Age* (2009). Forgetting used to be the default and digital storage flipped it; he proposes expiration dates for information. A concrete design answer to "help us forget".
- **Wendy Hui Kyong Chun**, *Updating to Remain the Same: Habitual New Media* (2016). Media matter most once they become habit, and habit is repetition. The media-studies side of Kolenc's repetition.
- **Lev Manovich**, *The Language of New Media* (2001). Database and narrative as "natural enemies": Simoniti's image and narrative in the vocabulary of software. A camera roll is a database; a film is a narrative.
- **Douglas Rushkoff**, *Present Shock: When Everything Happens Now* (2013). Presentism and the collapse of narrative, a popular precedent for instanternity with more everyday cases and less theory.
- **Byung-Chul Han**, *The Scent of Time* (2009) on time broken into points without duration, and *In the Swarm* (2013), *Infocracy* (2021) and *Non-things* (2021) on the digital crowd and information. Short books, close to her diagnosis, more essayistic than systematic.
- **Mark Fisher**, *Capitalist Realism* (2009) and *Ghosts of My Life* (2014), on the "slow cancellation of the future". The bleak counterpart to "a different internet is possible", and a fair test of it.
- **Shoshana Zuboff**, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019). How behavior becomes raw material and prediction becomes product: the documented mechanics behind "every post is capital".
- **Jonathan Crary**, *24/7: Late Capitalism and the Ends of Sleep* (2013) and *Scorched Earth* (2022). The always-on economy, and a harder line than hers: not a different internet, but a life beyond the internet complex.
- **Hito Steyerl**, "In Defense of the Poor Image" (*e-flux journal*, 2009). The compressed, copied image as the web's real currency: what happens to Simoniti's icon once it circulates.
- **The Ljubljana core**: Slavoj Žižek, *Hegel in a Wired Brain* (2020), on brain–computer interfaces and the subject; Alenka Zupančič, *What Is Sex?* (2017); Mladen Dolar, *A Voice and Nothing More* (2006). Where her vocabulary comes from.

## Further reading

**The subject**

- Lacan, "The Mirror Stage" (1936/1949, *Écrits*): why the self is an image and an object, behind her "the self is object to the subject".
- Lacan, *Seminar XI* (1964): "desidero is the Freudian cogito", the split subject, the big Other.
- Freud, "On Narcissism" (1914) and "A Difficulty in the Path of Psycho-Analysis" (1917), where the ego is "not master in its own house": the two ideas under her "real alien".
- Lichtenberg, *Waste Books*: say "it thinks", as one says "it lightens". The classic objection to "I think".
- Žižek, *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989), founding book of the Ljubljana school, which her abstract's "ideology without a sublime object" seems to nod to; *The Ticklish Subject* (1999) for the defence of the Cartesian subject.
- Kierkegaard, *Repetition* (1843) and *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844): repetition against recollection, and the moment where time touches eternity. Kolenc's *Repetition and Subjectivity: Kierkegaard, Freud, Lacan* (SUNY Press, 2026) is the bridge; I bought it after the panel.

**Time**

- Heidegger, *Being and Time* (1927), §81 on the "vulgar" concept of time, and the *Augenblick*: the two terms she borrows and turns around.
- Descartes, *Discourse on the Method* (1637), part two: the day in the heated room.
- Michael Levin, "Technological Approach to Mind Everywhere" (2022) and his lab's bioelectricity work: her non-temporal pattern.

**Media, capital, ideology**

- Debord, *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967), thesis 8, "reciprocal alienation". Short; read it whole.
- McLuhan, *Understanding Media: The Extensions of Man* (1964): the thesis she reverses.
- Marx, *Capital*, vol. 1, ch. 15 in English (13 in German), section 3b, "Prolongation of the Working-Day": moral depreciation, the "unfailing means" sentence, Aristotle's self-moving tripods.
- Althusser, "Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses" (1970): the school as dominant apparatus, to test her claim that the web replaced it.
- Badiou, *The Communist Hypothesis* (2009): the egalitarian emancipation behind "a different internet".
- Foucault, "What Is an Author?" (1969): authorship born with ownership.
- Deleuze and Guattari, *What Is Philosophy?* (1991), "Geophilosophy".
- Imperva, *Bad Bot Report* (2025): source of her 53%; check it before reusing the figure.

**Power, comedy, history**

- Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility" (1935/36), epilogue: fascism aestheticizes politics.
- Bergson, *Laughter* (1900): the mechanical encrusted on the living.
- Zupančič, *The Odd One In: On Comedy* (2008): the Ljubljana book on comedy, companion to the "random guy with a mustache".
- Fukuyama, *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992); Pinker, *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011): what Moder argued against.
- Shakespeare, *The Merchant of Venice* 3.1, read to the end of the speech; *Hamlet*.

**Film**

- The Kuleshov experiment (around 1920): one face, read as hunger, grief or desire by the next shot. Simoniti's minimum of narrative.
- Bordwell, *Narration in the Fiction Film* (1985): classical closure.
- *To Be or Not to Be* (Lubitsch, 1942), *The Great Dictator* (Chaplin, 1940).
- *The Searchers* (Ford, 1956), *Citizen Kane* (Welles, 1941): watch the last two minutes with Levi's question in mind.
- *Queen Margot* (Chéreau, 1994) against *Queen Christina* (Mamoulian, 1933).
- *La dolce vita* (Fellini, 1960), *Persona* (Bergman, 1966).
- *The 400 Blows* (Truffaut, 1959), *Run Lola Run* (Tykwer, 1998): the freeze and the loop.
